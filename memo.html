Signing up the long tail of small ISPs

Sofia Iuteri, Washington and Lee University · October 2026

The short version. About 2,500 small ISPs serve 1.5 to 2 million customers, and almost all of them have addresses their own network can't reach. They're too small for a traditional reseller program, and too many to sign up one sales call at a time. I think the fix has two parts. The first is an offer built for them: no minimums, they keep the customer, and the pitch is about the homes they can't serve, not about reselling a competitor. The second is an agent-driven motion that researches every ISP, writes outreach a person approves, and makes sign-up self-serve. I built a working version for Virginia and propose a 6-week pilot there.

The opportunity

Starlink is already the clear choice for rural and mobile customers. The hard part of reaching the rest of rural America is distribution, because many of those households trust a local provider that has served the county for decades. Those providers fall into four groups: fixed wireless ISPs, rural telephone companies and co-ops, electric co-ops that have moved into fiber, and small local fiber builders. Each has a few hundred to a few thousand customers, plus a list of homes it can't reach.

Why this has probably been hard

I don't know what happened with the earlier attempt, so these are my best guesses.

  1. The program was built for bigger partners. Public reports describe a reseller program that expected a business plan for millions in annual revenue and paused new sign-ups in 2026. An ISP with 800 customers can't meet that bar.
  2. The pitch sounds like "help your competitor." Many small ISPs are losing customers to Starlink. If the first message is "become a Starlink reseller," a lot of them won't answer.
  3. The math doesn't work for a person. One sales call per 800-customer company is an expensive way to win a few dozen subscribers. The research, outreach and onboarding have to cost close to nothing per ISP.
  4. Generic outreach gets ignored. These are tight-knit, local businesses. An email that shows you know their county and their network gets read. A template doesn't.

What the Virginia research shows

I researched 17 small Virginia providers from public sources: their own websites, state grant filings, and local news. A few patterns stood out:

  • 11 of 17 have an address checker or availability form. They already know exactly which homes they turn away.
  • 12 of 17 are in the middle of grant-funded builds (VATI, BEAD, USDA, CARES Act). Those builds focus on specific areas and leave the hardest addresses for later.
  • 8 of 17 say terrain or line of sight limits them, mostly in the Blue Ridge and Southwest Virginia.
  • 11 of 17 sell to businesses, so a Starlink backup line is an easy add-on sale.
  • One already sells Starlink on its own. Piedmont Broadband in Sperryville installs Starlink for $250 for homes that can't see its towers. It gets paid once, and the monthly relationship goes to Starlink. Some local ISPs are already doing this without any program, and a program would give them a reason to do more of it.

The offer

I'd suggest three tiers, so a 300-customer WISP and a 10,000-member co-op can both say yes:

TierWhat the ISP doesWhat it earns
ReferSends out-of-range addresses to a tracked Starlink linkA bounty per activation
InstallSells, installs and does first-line supportIts own install fee plus a monthly share
ResellBills the customer under its own brandA larger monthly share

Every tier would have no fees and no minimums, the ISP keeps the customer relationship, and the ISP can move a customer onto its own network later. Starlink would set the actual numbers. The prototype's calculator uses placeholder values.

The motion

  1. Find: start from the FCC's Broadband Data Collection, which lists every provider and the locations it serves, plus WISPA and NTCA member lists.
  2. Research: an agent profiles each ISP from public sources: coverage, technology, terrain, grants, address checkers, leadership. In the prototype this takes about a minute per company, and every fact links to its source.
  3. Rank: score each ISP on fit, with the reasons shown, so the team works the best ones first.
  4. Reach out: the agent drafts an email built on a real detail about the company. A person edits and approves every message.
  5. Convert: each ISP gets a personalized page with its own estimate, straight answers on channel conflict, and a 15-minute sign-up.
  6. Activate: training, a demo kit, and check-ins for partners who signed but haven't activated anyone yet.

Proposed pilot: Virginia, 6 weeks

  • Weeks 1 to 2: build the full list of Virginia providers from FCC data, profile and score all of them, and finalize offer terms with the team.
  • Weeks 3 to 5: approved outreach to the top 50, with calls handled by a person.
  • Week 6: results and a decision.

What I'd measure: reply rate, calls booked, partners signed, first activations, and research cost per ISP. A good result would show this works without a full-time salesperson per state. Then it rolls out state by state.

What only Starlink can answer

  • What tripped up the earlier attempt: the offer, the targeting, or the outreach?
  • What can partners actually earn today (bounty, install fee, monthly share), and who signs the agreement?
  • Is there a list of ISPs already contacted, or already reselling, that a pilot should start from or skip?
  • How does the team want to handle channel conflict in areas where Starlink sells direct?

What I've built so far

A working prototype: a Partner Console with 17 researched Virginia ISPs, fit scores and editable drafts, a live research agent that profiles any ISP you name, and a personalized partner page for each one. ISP details come from public sources and are linked. Earnings figures and program terms are placeholders until Starlink sets them.
